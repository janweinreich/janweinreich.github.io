Newtonian mechanics follows a trajectory. Hamiltonian mechanics asks a more
structural question: **what turns each observable into its rate of change?** For
familiar conservative systems the answer, $H$, is the total energy. More
generally, the Hamiltonian is the generator of time evolution.

Hamilton–Jacobi theory compresses a family of trajectories into one scalar field,
the action $S$. Quantum mechanics keeps the idea of generators, but replaces
phase-space functions by operators. This is why their equations rhyme so closely.

The equations below are meant to be read side by side. The symbol
$\longleftrightarrow$ marks a structural correspondence, not an identity between
classical and quantum mechanics.

## 1. The Hamiltonian generates motion

Start with a point $(q,p)$ in phase space. Hamilton's equations say

$$
\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},
\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}.
$$

Why do Poisson brackets appear? Take any classical observable $f(q,p,t)$ and
apply the ordinary chain rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{df}{dt}
&=
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
+\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i
+\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i
\right)\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
+\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}
-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The final sum is exactly the Poisson bracket

$$
\{f,H\}
=
\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}
-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}
\right),
$$

so all of Hamilton's equations fit into one line:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}
=
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\{f,H\}.
$$

The quantum version starts from a different intuition: probabilities must remain
probabilities, so time evolution is unitary. During a tiny interval $dt$,

$$
U(dt)=1-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat H\,dt+O(dt^2).
$$

Move the time dependence from the state into an observable. To first order,

$$
U^\dagger(dt)\,\hat f\,U(dt)
=
\hat f+\frac{dt}{i\hbar}[\hat f,\hat H]+O(dt^2).
$$

Dividing by $dt$ gives the Heisenberg equation:

$$
\frac{d\hat f}{dt}
=
\frac{\partial \hat f}{\partial t}
+\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat f,\hat H].
$$

The commutator measures how much “apply $\hat f$, then evolve” differs from
“evolve, then apply $\hat f$.” If $[\hat f,\hat H]=0$ and $\hat f$ has no explicit
time dependence, it is conserved. The identical statement classically is
$\{f,H\}=0$.

$$
\boxed{
\{\,\cdot,\cdot\,\}
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\,\cdot,\cdot\,]
}
$$

## 2. Canonical pairs already contain the dictionary

The classical relation follows immediately from the definition of the Poisson
bracket. For example,

$$
\{q_i,p_j\}
=
\sum_k\left(
\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial q_k}
\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial p_k}
-
\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial p_k}
\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial q_k}
\right)
=\delta_{ij}.
$$

Therefore the phase-space coordinates satisfy

$$
\{q_i,q_j\}=0,
\qquad
\{p_i,p_j\}=0,
\qquad
\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}.
$$

To see the quantum relation just as directly, ask what moves a wavefunction by a
small distance $a$. A translation acts as

$$
(T(a)\psi)(q)=\psi(q-a)
\approx
\psi(q)-a\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial q}.
$$

Writing the same translation as

$$
T(a)=e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar}
\approx
1-\frac{ia}{\hbar}\hat p
$$

identifies the momentum operator:

$$
\hat p=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial q}.
$$

Now let the position–momentum commutator act on any wavefunction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\hat q,\hat p]\psi
&=
q(-i\hbar\psi')+i\hbar(q\psi)'\\
&=i\hbar\psi.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since this holds for every $\psi$, $[\hat q,\hat p]=i\hbar$. In several
dimensions, the full set is

$$
[\hat q_i,\hat q_j]=0,
\qquad
[\hat p_i,\hat p_j]=0,
\qquad
[\hat q_i,\hat p_j]=i\hbar\delta_{ij}.
$$

At the canonical level, the factor $i\hbar$ converts the classical relation into
the quantum one. The commutator is not an arbitrary rule added afterward: it is
already encoded in the fact that momentum generates translations.

## 3. Generators have the same grammar

Time evolution is only one example of a continuous transformation. Let
$\varepsilon$ be any transformation parameter and let $G$ be its generator.
Classically, moving a tiny distance along the flow generated by $G$ gives

$$
\frac{df}{d\varepsilon}=\{f,G\}.
$$

Quantum mechanically, the corresponding unitary transformation is

$$
U(\varepsilon)=e^{-i\varepsilon\hat G/\hbar}.
$$

The same first-order expansion used for time evolution gives

$$
\frac{d\hat f}{d\varepsilon}
=
\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat f,\hat G].
$$

Side by side:

$$
\delta f=\varepsilon\{f,G\}
\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad
\delta\hat f=\frac{\varepsilon}{i\hbar}[\hat f,\hat G].
$$

The same pattern appears repeatedly:

| Generator | Transformation |
| --- | --- |
| $H$ | time evolution |
| $p_i$ | translation in $q_i$ |
| $L_i$ | rotation about axis $i$ |

For instance, choosing $G=p_j$ gives

$$
\delta q_i
=
\varepsilon\{q_i,p_j\}
=
\varepsilon\delta_{ij},
$$

which is a translation. Classically these flows are canonical transformations;
quantum mechanically they are unitary transformations. The generator keeps its
role while the surrounding algebra changes.

## 4. Hamilton–Jacobi action becomes quantum phase

Hamilton–Jacobi theory asks whether the information in a whole family of
trajectories can be stored in a single function. Fix an initial point and define
$S(q,t)$ as the action evaluated along the classical path ending at $(q,t)$:

$$
S(q,t)
=
\int_{(q_0,t_0)}^{(q,t)}
\left(p\cdot dq-H\,dt\right).
$$

Move only the endpoint a little. Because the path in the interior is already a
classical path and therefore a stationary point of the action, its small deformation contributes
nothing at first order. The first-order change comes entirely from the endpoint:

$$
dS=\sum_i p_i\,dq_i-H\,dt.
$$

Now describe exactly the same change by treating $S(q,t)$ as an ordinary
function of its endpoint coordinates. Its total differential is

$$
dS
=
\sum_i\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i}\,dq_i
+\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}\,dt.
$$

We therefore have two expressions for the same $dS$:

$$
\underbrace{
\sum_i\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i}\,dq_i
+\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}\,dt
}_{\text{ordinary differential of }S}
=
\underbrace{
\sum_i p_i\,dq_i-H\,dt
}_{\text{endpoint change of the action}}.
$$

The variations $dq_1,dq_2,\ldots$ and $dt$ are independent: we may move the
final position while holding the final time fixed, or move the final time while
holding the position fixed. The equality must hold for every such choice, so
the coefficient of each independent variation must match:

$$
p_i=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i},
\qquad
H=-\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}.
$$

The first relation says that moving the endpoint in the $q_i$ direction changes
the action at a rate equal to the final momentum $p_i$. The second says that
extending the endpoint in time changes the action at a rate equal to minus the
Hamiltonian.

At the endpoint, the Hamiltonian is a function $H(q,p,t)$. Write the second
relation with those arguments shown explicitly:

$$
-\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}=H(q,p,t).
$$

The first relation tells us every component of the momentum appearing on the
right:

$$
p_i=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
p=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}.
$$

Substitute this expression for $p$ into $H(q,p,t)$:

$$
-\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}
=
H\!\left(q,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},t\right).
$$

Finally, bring both terms to one side. This is the Hamilton–Jacobi equation:

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}
+H\!\left(q,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},t\right)=0.
$$

This makes $S$ an action landscape. Its level sets are wavefronts, and
$p=\nabla S$ says that momentum points normal to them.

Why should the same $S$ appear in quantum mechanics? Start with the phase of an
ordinary plane wave:

$$
e^{i(k\cdot q-\omega t)}.
$$

The quantity $k\cdot q-\omega t$ is an angle, so it must be dimensionless. Now
return to the endpoint relation for the classical action and divide it by
$\hbar$:

$$
d\!\left(\frac{S}{\hbar}\right)
=
\frac{p}{\hbar}\cdot dq
-\frac{H}{\hbar}\,dt.
$$

Define the local wavevector and angular frequency by

$$
k=\frac{p}{\hbar},
\qquad
\omega=\frac{H}{\hbar}.
$$

For the conservative systems considered here, $H=E$, so these are precisely the
de Broglie and Planck relations, $p=\hbar k$ and $E=\hbar\omega$. The action
relation then reads

$$
d\!\left(\frac{S}{\hbar}\right)
=
k\cdot dq-\omega\,dt,
$$

which is the local change of a wave's phase. In the simple case of constant
momentum and energy,

$$
S(q,t)=p\cdot q-Et+S_0,
$$

and therefore

$$
e^{iS/\hbar}
=
e^{iS_0/\hbar}e^{i(k\cdot q-\omega t)}.
$$

This is the familiar plane wave, apart from one constant phase. Thus $S/\hbar$ is not
chosen merely because it has no units. Its spatial slope is the wavevector and
its temporal slope is minus the angular frequency.

There is also a composition reason. If a path consists of two consecutive
segments, their actions add, $S=S_1+S_2$, while their phase factors multiply:

$$
e^{i(S_1+S_2)/\hbar}
=
e^{iS_1/\hbar}e^{iS_2/\hbar}.
$$

Different paths acquire different phases, so their action difference
$\Delta S$ becomes the relative phase $\Delta S/\hbar$ that controls
interference. The natural phase factor associated with the classical action is
therefore

$$
\phi_S(q,t)=\exp\!\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S(q,t)\right).
$$

The momentum and energy operators simply read the spatial and temporal slopes of
this phase:

$$
-i\hbar\nabla\phi_S
=(\nabla S)\phi_S,
\qquad
i\hbar\frac{\partial\phi_S}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}\phi_S.
$$

So the Hamilton–Jacobi identifications sit directly beside the quantum operators:

$$
\boxed{
p=\nabla S
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\hat p=-i\hbar\nabla
}
\qquad
\boxed{
E=-\partial_t S
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\hat E=i\hbar\partial_t
}.
$$

There is an even sharper connection. For one particle with
$H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$, the Schrödinger equation is

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
=
\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V\right)\psi.
$$

Insert the simplest phase wave, $\psi=e^{iS/\hbar}$. Its two sides become

$$
i\hbar\partial_t\psi
=
-(\partial_t S)\psi,
$$

and

$$
\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V\right)\psi
=
\left[
\frac{(\nabla S)^2}{2m}+V
-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2S
\right]\psi.
$$

When the local momentum varies slowly enough that
$|\hbar\nabla^2S|\ll|(\nabla S)^2|$, the term explicitly proportional to
$\hbar$ is subleading. What remains is

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}
+\frac{(\nabla S)^2}{2m}+V=0,
$$

exactly the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. More generally one writes
$\psi\sim a\,e^{iS/\hbar}$; the next terms transport the amplitude $a$, while
interference appears when multiple phase branches are superposed.

## 5. The commutator returns to phase space

The final correspondence becomes especially transparent in the phase-space
formulation of quantum mechanics. Under the Weyl correspondence, ordinary
operator multiplication becomes a deformed multiplication of phase-space
functions. For one canonical pair it is the star product

$$
A\star B
=
A\exp\!\left[
\frac{i\hbar}{2}
\left(
\overleftarrow\partial_q\overrightarrow\partial_p
-\overleftarrow\partial_p\overrightarrow\partial_q
\right)
\right]B.
$$

The arrows only say which factor is differentiated. Expanding the exponential
starts with

$$
A\star B
=
AB+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\{A,B\}+O(\hbar^2),
$$

while reversing $A$ and $B$ flips the sign of the Poisson-bracket term. All even
powers cancel in the difference, so

$$
A\star B-B\star A
=
i\hbar\{A,B\}+O(\hbar^3).
$$

Define the Moyal bracket by dividing this difference by $i\hbar$:

$$
\{A,B\}_{\mathrm M}
=
\frac{A\star B-B\star A}{i\hbar}
=
\{A,B\}+O(\hbar^2).
$$

The operator statement and the phase-space statement are exactly paired:

$$
\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat A,\hat B]
\quad\stackrel{\mathrm{Weyl}}{\longleftrightarrow}\quad
\{A,B\}_{\mathrm M}.
$$

This closes the circle. Quantum noncommutativity becomes a controlled deformation
of ordinary multiplication in phase space, and its first correction away from
commuting multiplication is precisely the Poisson bracket.

## The correspondence at a glance

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\text{Hamiltonian flow}
&\longleftrightarrow&
\text{Hamilton–Jacobi wavefronts}
\\[4pt]
\dot f=\{f,H\}
&&
p=\nabla S
\\[10pt]
&\searrow\qquad\swarrow&
\\[-2pt]
&\psi\sim a\,e^{iS/\hbar}&
\\[8pt]
&\dot{\hat f}=\dfrac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat f,\hat H]&
\end{array}
$$

## Where the analogy stops

- The Hamilton–Jacobi equation is nonlinear in $S$; the Schrödinger equation is
  linear in $\psi$.
- The form $\psi\sim a\,e^{iS/\hbar}$ is a semiclassical approximation, not a
  representation of every quantum state by one classical action.
- Replacing every Poisson bracket by a commutator is not a universal quantization
  rule. Beyond special classes of observables, operator ordering matters and no
  map preserves every classical product and bracket.

The recurring idea is not that the three theories are the same. It is that
dynamics is expressed through a generator: as a flow on phase space, as an
action whose level sets form wavefronts, or as unitary motion in Hilbert space.

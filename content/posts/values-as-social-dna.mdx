Where do personal values come from?

The question sounds simple until we try to define a value in the first place.
There is no mathematical definition that settles the matter. We can at least
agree that people hold different opinions, and that political opinions are often
rooted in ideologies. I take from Slavoj Žižek, admittedly in a very amateur
way, the idea that ideology can present the reality we would like to have rather
than the reality we are actually living in.

Ideologies, in turn, rest on values with which individuals feel connected. That
only moves the question one level deeper: where do those values come from?

## The conditions before the choice

Perhaps even asking about values is a symptom of a particular kind of society:
one with enough time and material security to worry about values at all.

Think of a society struggling under repression. “Freedom” is itself difficult to
define, but there may be neither the liberty nor the practical space to choose
among abstract values. If you do not have the resources to feed your family, you
do not have the same time or freedom to examine which values you would like to
pursue.

Values are therefore connected to socio-economic background. Some may be
possible only under particular systems or conditions.

In a capitalist society, for example, accumulating wealth can become a value. It
might mean less in a society organized differently. Wealth has existed through
much of recorded history, especially since the rise of class societies, but not
on the full scale of human evolution. More complex class societies created
civilizations in which norms developed independently and appeared in different
forms. Wealth became recognizable, objectified, and represented by money or
physical possession.

Now return to one person inside such a society. Their socio-economic background
creates boundary conditions around the values available to them. A person
focused on feeding a family is unlikely to pursue extreme wealth for its own
sake. Other values may never become realistic possibilities because the
conditions do not allow for them.

Is the whole ensemble of values a person can pursue already partly defined from
the outset? Are the surrounding constraints defining not merely which values are
practical, but which values the person can imagine adopting at all?

## Can a value be inherent?

If values arise from a person's background and their interaction with the
environment, what would a genuinely inherent value be? What could someone
discover and follow that did not come from society?

Set aside godlike beings from which values might be derived. Religion can then
also be considered a principle or social effect through which a person finds
values. The radical conclusion is that there may be no inherent value position
available to an individual.

If we still want to defend the possibility of an independent value system, we
seem to have two choices: either admit that complete independence is impossible,
or make the rejection of inherited social values itself the guiding value. One
would have to try to create one's own values.

This is not a new idea. It recalls *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* by Friedrich
Nietzsche. Zarathustra withdraws into the mountains and, in isolation, attempts
to discover values apart from society. When he descends, he finds humanity still
attached to its old ideas and ideologies.

But complete isolation cannot be the only route to an independent value. Even
if an epiphany arrived in isolation, a wider society might be unable to
understand it. Outside the language of shared norms, the new value could look
like random thoughts or words alien to the world that must receive them.

Values can also emerge through experience and through contact with nature.
Extreme events can make a person question the social values they inherited.
Illness or suffering can do the same when accepting those values becomes too
difficult. These are not values present at birth; they appear along the
trajectory of a life.

## The social genome

Once a new value exists, it behaves like a mutation in what we might call the
DNA of a society.

The complete set of values resembles a social gene pool. Particular values are
expressed more strongly in some societies and more weakly in others. Different
phenotypes give different outward expressions to similar values. Taken
together, these expressions give rise to ideology.

The function of this social DNA is to maintain order and help a society continue
to work within its environment. But its values necessarily lag behind the real
technological and social environment. They can seem suboptimal if the goal of
policy is the overall benefit of the society, because the inherited values
describe a population that no longer exists in quite the same form. Adaptation
takes time.

What role does the individual play in that process?

An independently discovered value initially looks like a random mutation. In a
local sense, it differs from the values already expressed around it. It may not
be useful to society at all, even if it is deeply valuable to the person in whom
it appeared. But if similar mutations accumulate, the social genome can
eventually flip.

## The irony of acceptance

Here is the central irony.

An individual or group contributes a new value, intentionally or otherwise. If
the value succeeds, it becomes part of society's core DNA. Once there, it starts
doing what the old values did: it helps maintain social order, and it limits the
space in which later individuals can discover values independently.

The value is authentic or revolutionary at the moment it enters the social
core. Immediately afterward, it is inherited. Its originality is lost at the
moment of acceptance.

An individual value therefore seems able to remain fully individual only if it
never has a lasting impact. If it does spread, it changes the shared value
system, but in doing so becomes another condition imposed on the people who
follow.

That is the struggle: an inherent value seeks expression in society, yet the
moment it is successfully expressed, it begins to lose the independence that
made it inherent.

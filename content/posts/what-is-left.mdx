A friend told me his university would not reimburse the AI subscriptions he uses for work. His peers use these tools to write grants, proposals, and publications. He can pay for them himself, use something cheaper, or go without.

The university can treat the subscription as an optional expense. From the researcher's side, it is harder to treat as optional when other people are using the tool to compete for the same funding. The institution can decline the bill; the individual still has to find a way to keep up.

I keep thinking about what that bill might eventually cover. As an AI system takes on more of an investigation, paying for it could mean buying the effort of pursuing an idea. A decision about software expenses would begin to determine how much of a question someone gets to explore.

## How long can you keep going?

Imagine two researchers investigating the same question. Both are good at their work. One has to ration each run of an automated research system. The other can explore many approaches at once and let them continue overnight. The extra runs may come to nothing. But one researcher gets to test possibilities that the other has to leave alone.

The scale need not be imaginary. OpenAI [describes coordinating roughly 10,000 agents at once](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/) in a mathematics research project. That is its account of the resources it used, rather than a measure of how effective each agent was. It illustrates how little “both researchers use AI” might tell us about their actual circumstances.

If useful progress can be bought this way, an idea comes with a running cost. Knowing what to try next and being able to pay for the next attempt become separate things.

I have [Decascope](https://uni-versum.netlify.app/) and [Dibs on Mars!](https://dibs-on-mars.vercel.app/) linked on this blog, both vibe-coded for fun. That is a use of AI I can choose for myself. The same tools can help someone attempt a project they could not have taken on alone. Choosing help for something you want to make is a different situation from having to delegate work to remain competitive.

Being more capable does not guarantee a stronger position. A researcher might do far better work with affordable AI and still lose ground to a group that can afford much more. For a particular grant or job, what matters is how the applicants compare. Cheaper tools can broaden participation without removing the advantage at the expensive end.

## When the route is the work

GPS is a useful comparison up to a point. It saves the work of finding a route, which is often exactly what someone wants. In research, finding a route can be much of what makes the activity worth doing.

A failed approach can teach you something you would never have thought to ask about. Noticing why another approach might work can be the part you remember long after the result. If I delegate the search and receive a finished answer, I have skipped something that mattered to me, even if the explanation is excellent.

AI assistance can also keep someone involved in more ambitious work. The uncomfortable case is where I would prefer to spend time working something out, but cannot justify the slower approach to whoever decides whether the work gets funded. I may get a better result while having less say in which parts of the process I get to do.

Mathematics makes the social side of that change particularly visible. Checking a proof and discovering one are different experiences. A community also develops around people explaining why they tried an approach, defending a choice, or disagreeing about what should come next. A conference where researchers exchange those experiences and one where they try to catch up with a batch of machine-produced proofs would be different kinds of gathering. Both might be useful. They would offer different working lives.

I still want to work things out, even when a system can reach an answer sooner. Understanding something can remain satisfying after someone else has solved it. What troubles me is the possibility that this becomes an activity people are encouraged to keep for their spare time, while their careers reward delegating more of it.

Keeping that pleasure as a hobby is a different prospect from building a life around it.

## A question with a running cost

Research has always depended on money. The possible change is how far that dependence reaches into the act of thinking. A person could learn enough to identify a promising problem and still be unable to afford a serious attempt at it. Some intellectual work that once needed relatively little capital could acquire a substantial operating cost.

That cost has a physical basis. These systems need electricity, hardware, and people to run them. Better algorithms can reduce the expense, but useful computation still has to be supplied by someone. When it becomes part of how ideas are pursued, the people who control it acquire influence over what gets explored.

A well-funded lab could turn more attempts into results, use those results to secure funding, and buy more attempts. A company could do the same with revenue. The loop is strongest where the returns stay with those already able to pay. Sharing methods and making useful systems widely available could spread the benefit instead. How this is organized will matter as much as whether the models improve.

Renting access is what makes much of that infrastructure usable without owning it. Independence need not mean owning a data center. It does mean having a way to continue your work if a provider changes its prices, withdraws a model, or becomes difficult to replace. A low subscription fee today tells us little about how much control someone will retain over a project that lasts years.

I keep coming back to my friend's bill. His university has made a decision about an expense. If the service eventually does substantial parts of an investigation, that decision could reach further into what he can attempt and how he spends his working life.

I want learning to remain a way into this work. It would be a strange outcome if AI made it possible to ask almost any question, while making the freedom to follow one through depend more heavily on what we can afford.
